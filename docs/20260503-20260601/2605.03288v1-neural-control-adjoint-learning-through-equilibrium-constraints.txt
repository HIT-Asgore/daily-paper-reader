## Abstract 

Many physical AI tasks are governed by implicit equilibrium: an agent actuates a subset of degrees of freedom (boundary DoFs), while the remain-ing free DoFs settle by minimizing a total po-tential energy. Even seemingly basic tasks such as bending a deformable linear object (DLO) to a target shape can exhibit strongly nonlinear be-havior due to multi-stability: the same boundary conditions may yield multiple equilibrium shapes depending on the actuation trajectory. However, learning and control in such systems is brittle because the actuation-to-configuration map is de-fined only implicitly, and naive backpropagation through iterative equilibrium solvers is memory-and compute-intensive. We propose Neural Con-trol, a boundary-control framework that computes trajectory-dependent, memory-efficient proxy gra-dients by differentiating equilibrium conditions via an adjoint formulation, avoiding unrolling of solver iterations. To improve robustness over long horizons, we integrate these sensitivities into a receding-horizon MPC scheme that repeatedly re-anchors optimization to realized equilibria and mitigates basin-switching in multi-stable regimes. We evaluate Neural Control in simulation and on physical robots manipulating DLOs, and show im-proved performance over gradient-free baselines such as SPSA and CEM.

## 2. Related works 

Optimization and learning for long-horizon, path-dependent control. Long-horizon control and sequential decision-making are central themes in machine learning, where performance is often limited by delayed credit as-signment and partial observability, and, in model-based settings, by compounding model error. A common re-sponse is to cast history dependence as a partially observable Markov decision process (POMDP) and augment policies and value functions with memory, e.g., recurrent reinforce-ment learning (RL) (Hausknecht & Stone, 2015), or to learn latent state-space models that support planning via imag-ined rollouts (Hafner et al., 2019b;a). Another dominant paradigm is model-based RL with receding-horizon plan-ning: actions are optimized over a short horizon and re-peatedly replanned using model predictive control (MPC) to reduce error accumulation and improve robustness un-der regime changes (Chua et al., 2018). Complementary to these approaches, black-box and sampling-based opti-mizers remain strong baselines for long-horizon problems because they avoid differentiating through complex transi-tions and can explore multi-modal outcomes. Representa-tive examples include simultaneous perturbation stochas-tic approximation (SPSA) (Spall, 2002), the cross-entropy method (CEM) (Botev et al., 2013), and parameter-space search methods such as evolution strategies (ES) (Salimans et al., 2017) and augmented random search (ARS) (Mania et al., 2018). Recent work also makes CEM differentiable (DCEM) (Amos & Yarats, 2020). However, the rollout cost of these methods typically grows quickly with hori-zon length and action dimension, which becomes especially acute when each step involves an expensive implicit equi-librium computation (Pinneri et al., 2021). Our method retains receding-horizon replanning but computes physics-informed, trajectory-level sensitivities through implicit tran-sitions along the realized branch, enabling efficient gradient-based optimization. 

Memory-efficient, trajectory-based gradients for long horizons. Backpropagation through time (BPTT) pro-vides a direct way to obtain long-horizon, trajectory-dependent gradients by propagating sensitivities across timesteps (Williams & Peng, 1990), but naively storing intermediate states makes memory scale with horizon length, motivating checkpointing and recomputation strate-gies (Gruslys et al., 2016). In differentiable physics and control, this challenge becomes more pronounced when each transition is an implicit update solved via inner it-erations (e.g., Newton or conjugate gradient), since dif-ferentiating through solver iterations is memory-intensive and can be numerically brittle near instabilities (Amos & Kolter, 2017; Suh et al., 2022). Inspired by adjoint sensi-tivity methods (Chen et al., 2018), a complementary line of work avoids unrolling by differentiating through implicit functions via optimality or fixed-point conditions, including deep equilibrium models (Bai et al., 2019) and differentiable implicit layers (Look et al., 2020). Related ideas also appear in differentiable control, such as differentiable MPC via KKT conditions (Amos et al., 2018) and Pontryagin differ-entiable programming (PDP) (Jin et al., 2020). In contrast 2Neural Control: Adjoint Learning Through Equilibrium Constraints 

to DEQ/implicit layers that differentiate a single fixed point, we address long-horizon optimization through sequences of equilibria where multi-stability makes the realized trajectory branch-dependent. In contrast to differentiable MPC/PDP, our setting requires sensitivities through an implicit equi-librium solve at every step rather than explicit dynamics or a single KKT system. Compared to BPTT through solver iterations, we avoid unrolling inner solves and use a proxy-adjoint recursion; we further adopt a frozen-tangent proxy to avoid higher-order derivatives along long horizons. 

Differentiable models for learning and control. Differ-entiable modeling and simulation have become core prim-itives for gradient-based learning, planning, and inverse problems, and recent ML toolchains such as DiffTaichi (Hu et al., 2019a) and Brax (Freeman et al., 2021) make opti-mizing through physics practical at scale. Differentiable simulators have been extended from rigid bodies to de-formables and soft bodies, including ChainQueen (Hu et al., 2019b), DiffPD (Du et al., 2021), and differentiable soft multi-body simulation (Qiao et al., 2021). For flexible struc-tures, discrete differential geometry (DDG) discretizations provide geometry-preserving, energy-based models with analytic derivatives (Bergou et al., 2008; Grinspun et al., 2003; Huang et al., 2025). Benchmarks further clarify the difficulty of long-horizon deformable manipulation, e.g., SoftGym (Lin et al., 2021) and PlasticineLab (Huang et al., 2021). Learned differentiable simulators can offer fast pre-dictors, but near multistability and bifurcation boundaries deterministic models can blur branch selection without ex-plicit multimodality (e.g., mixture formulations) (Sanchez-Gonzalez et al., 2020; Pfaff et al., 2021; Bishop, 1994). Recent work also integrates analytic gradients from dif-ferentiable multiphysics simulations into RL updates (e.g., SAPO) (Xing et al., 2025). Our work complements these efforts by focusing on the gradient pathway itself: memory-efficient, trajectory-level sensitivities through sequences of implicit equilibrium solves so optimization follows the real-ized branch. 

## 3. Methodology 

We study learning and control problems where the system state is defined implicitly by an equilibrium condition rather than an explicit dynamics model. A continuation parameter 

λ ∈ [0 , 1] indexes the process, with λ = 0 the initial con-figuration and λ = 1 the terminal configuration. At each λ,the equilibrium state x(λ) is obtained by solving 

G(x(λ), z(λ)) = 0, (1) where z(λ) denotes controllable quantities entering the equi-librium (e.g., boundary conditions, loads, or design param-eters), rather than by an explicit state update of the form 

xk+1 = f (xk). In mechanics, Equation (1) corresponds to Newton’s second law in the quasi-static regime, where inertia and vibrational effects are neglected and internal con-servative forces balance applied loads. A common instance is 

G(x, z) = ∇xE(x, z) − Fext , (2) where E is total potential energy and Fext collects external loads. Equilibrium-governed systems pose two challenges: (i) x(λ)

is typically computed by iterative solvers (e.g., Newton– CG), so differentiating through solver iterations across long horizons is expensive; and (ii) equilibria can be multi-stable, so for fixed z, multiple stable solutions may exist and warm-started solvers follow a realized branch through their basins of attraction. We therefore generate rollouts by warm-started equilibrium solves along the continuation path. A neural controller parameterized by Θ produces a continu-ation control signal 

u(λ) = uΘ(λ), (3) which drives z(λ) via known continuation dynamics

3.3. Receding-horizon continuation control 

Over long continuations, proxy/discretization errors can accumulate, and in multi-stable regions overly aggressive updates may drive the equilibrium solve across basins of attraction. ## 4. Results 

We evaluate our method on elastic-strip manipulation tasks (Figure 1A). The strip undergoes large deformations and 4Neural Control: Adjoint Learning Through Equilibrium Constraints

We con-sider three tasks that progressively stress these effects: (i) point-to-point manipulation (terminal objective), (ii) path following of a designated strip point (trajectory objective), and (iii) shape formation in multi-stable regimes (branch selection). All methods share the same forward rollout, which com-putes strip configurations by solving implicit equilibria to convergence at each continuation step. We compare our full method (Adjoint+RHC) against SPSA and CEM, and ab-late our approach by removing RHC (Adjoint-only). Each method is run for the same number of parameter-update steps T (optimizer updates on Θ). An objective evalua-tion corresponds to one full rollout that computes the task loss from implicit equilibrium solves. Because SPSA uses two rollouts per update (two-sided perturbations) and CEM uses P rollouts per update (population evaluation), these gradient-free methods consume multiple objective evalua-tions per update. Adjoint-only and Adjoint+RHC use one rollout per update and additionally solve tangent/adjoint linear systems to obtain gradients, avoiding backpropaga-tion through the Newton/CG iterations of the equilibrium solver. We therefore report both best achieved loss and total wall-clock time, reflecting rollout cost and gradient over-head. Finally, we validate the approach in real-world robotic experiments. 

4.1. Experimental setup 

We model the manipulated object as a planar elastic strip with stretching and bending energy. At each continuation step, the forward rollout computes the realized configuration by solving the corresponding equilibrium equations to con-vergence under imposed boundary constraints. We partition degrees of freedom into boundary nodes qb and free nodes 

qf and set z ≡ qb, x ≡ qf (Figure 1B): control acts only through boundary conditions, while free nodes are solved implicitly and used to evaluate task losses. The strip is held by clamped boundary conditions at both ends; Tasks 1–2 actuate transverse (global x-axis in our setup) displacements at both ends, and Task 3 actuates (x, y ) displacement and in-plane rotation at one end with the other end passively clamped. Unless otherwise stated, we discretize the strip with N = 101 nodes and ignore gravity in numerical ex-periments. The stretching and bending stiffness are fixed to 

ks = 0 .31 N and kb = 7 .85 × 10 −8 N m 2. Full discretiza-tion and solver details are provided in the Appendix B.

4.5. Real-world experiments 

As shown in Fig. 5, a 6-DoF robot arm actuates one endpoint of an elastic strip, while the other endpoint is fixed using a passive clamp. The strip has measured length ≈25 cm, width 0.5 cm, and thickness 0.127 mm. ## 5. Discussion 

Our results highlight two recurring difficulties in equilibrium-governed control: (i) obtaining usable gradients when states are defined only implicitly by a converged equi-librium solve, and (ii) maintaining branch consistency over long continuations in multi-stable landscapes. We address (i) by keeping the forward pass exact (each rollout state is a converged equilibrium) while computing proxy-adjoint gradients that avoid differentiating through Newton–CG it-erations. Despite the frozen-tangent approximation, these gradients are sufficiently informative to optimize terminal and trajectory objectives. We address (ii) with RHC, which localizes proxy/discretization error to short segments and re-peatedly re-anchors optimization at realized equilibria. This wrapper is most beneficial in multi-stable regimes, where it reduces basin drift and improves reliability of branch selection. Limitations include frozen-tangent bias (ignored dS/dλ and higher-order terms), reduced reliability near bifurcations or under non-smooth physics, and limited lookahead in segment-wise updates, which may require stronger terminal objectives or shaping to solve long-horizon tasks. Equilib-rium solves remain the dominant cost, motivating better warm-starting, preconditioning, and adaptive step sizing. 

## 6. Conclusion 

We presented proxy-adjoint neural continuation for control and learning in equilibrium-defined systems, combining exact equilibrium rollouts with efficient implicit sensitiv-ities and a receding-horizon continuation (RHC) wrapper for robust long-horizon branch tracking. Empirically, Ad-joint+RHC achieves large efficiency gains: it is 19–127 ×

faster than SPSA/CEM across Tasks 1–3 while reaching 3–6 orders of magnitude lower best loss on the same fixed update budgets. Finally, we demonstrate qualitative sim-to-real transfer on a 6-DoF robotic setup under gravity and experimentally identified material parameters (Figure 5), indicating that proxy-adjoint continuation is a practical tool for real manipulation of compliant, multi-stable structures. 8Neural Control: Adjoint Learning Through Equilibrium Constraints

## D. Implementation details and hyperparameters 

This section reports the hyperparameters used in our numer-ical experiments (Secs. 4.3–4.4). Unless otherwise stated, all methods share the same forward equilibrium simulator and the same controller parameterization; they differ only in the optimizer. 

Elastic-strip simulator and equilibrium solver. We dis-cretize the strip centerline into N +1 = 101 nodes and clamp the first two and last two nodes. Unless otherwise stated, material parameters are kb = 7 .85 × 10 −8 N · m2

and ks = 0 .314 N , and we set g = 0 (no gravity) in all numerical experiments. At each continuation step we solve for the free DoFs using a trust-region Newton–CG method (App. B), warm-started from the previous equilibrium. We use initial radius ∆0 =10 −3, acceptance threshold η = 0 .1, shrink/expand factors 

(0 .5, 2.0) , and at most 200 outer iterations. We terminate when ∥∇ Etot ∥ ≤ 10 −9 (gradient w.r.t. free DoFs) or when the radius drops below 10 −8.If the Hessian is detected SPD we take a Newton/dogleg step; otherwise we use Steihaug PCG in the trust region. PCG is capped at 200 iterations and stops when the PCG residual satisfies ∥r∥ ≤ min(0 .5, p∥∇ E∥)∥∇ E∥, on neg-ative curvature, or upon hitting the trust-region boundary. We use a diagonal (Jacobi) preconditioner. 

Controller parameterization and optimization. We parameterize the continuation controller uΘ(λ) as alightweight MLP that takes the scalar λ ∈ [0 , 1] as input and outputs the control vector u(λ) ∈ Rdu . For Tasks 1–2, 

du = 2 (left/right transverse displacement rates); for Task 3, 

du = 3 (end-effector (x, y ) displacement rates and in-plane rotation rate). Unless otherwise stated, we use 2–3 hidden layers with width 64 (architectures [64 , 64] or [64 , 64 , 64] ), ReLU activations, and a final tanh to bound the output; we scale the output to enforce ∥u(λ)∥ ≤ umax .For the adjoint-only baseline, we use a single rollout with full horizon K = 101 continuation steps. For RHC+adjoint, we use segment horizon H = 10 continuation steps per segment (with horizon shifting as described in Sec. 3.3). All numerical experiments use Adam with learning rate 10 −2

to optimize Θ, and we keep the optimizer settings identical across methods. 

Baseline hyperparameters. We use population size 

Npop = 10 , elite fraction 0.3, smoothing α = 0 .25 , and standard deviation settings σ0 = 0 .5 umax , with a minimum floor σmin = 10 −2. We choose Npop = 10 because the per-step action is low-dimensional ( dim( u) ∈ { 2, 3} depending on the task), so a modest population is typically sufficient for exploration. 

SPSA. We use learning rate 10 −2, perturbation magnitude 

c = 5 × 10 −3, and average m = 2 independent SPSA per-turbation pairs per update. We choose a relatively large c

to obtain a stable finite-difference directional signal under strongly nonlinear deformation; with unit-normalized strip length, c = 5 × 10 −3 corresponds to a small ( ∼ 0.5% )geometric perturbation that empirically yields more reli-able search directions than smaller perturbations that can be dominated by solver tolerance and numerical noise.
